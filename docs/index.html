The short edition of Jeremiah at Qumran

ירמיהו

Jeremiah comes down in two editions. The Masoretic Text is about an eighth longer than the text the Greek translator worked from, and in places it puts the chapters in another order. Of the six Jeremiah scrolls from the Judaean Desert, four go with the Masoretic Text. Two do not.

This page sets those two, 4QJerᵇ and 4QJerᵈ, against the Masoretic Text and the Greek, and keeps three things apart throughout: what is extant, what a modern editor restored within a lacuna, and what follows from the extent of the lacuna. Only 10% of the first manuscript and 45% of the second is extant.

Jeremiah 10 is not in the same order in all three

The Masoretic chapter runs straight through. The Greek has no verses 6 to 8 and no verse 10, and it prints verse 9 before verse 5. The scroll does the same, and splits verse 5 around it.

Two editors say the same thing about verses 6, 7, 8 and 10 independently. Rahlfs simply has no such verses. Stipp, setting the two editions side by side in columns, leaves the alexandrian column of all four of them empty, which is his statement that the Old Greek has nothing answering to them at all. And his apparatus names 4Q71 at 10:6 and 10:10 — he read this manuscript here too.

Line 5 ends with הב ייפהו במקבות, from Jeremiah 10:4. Line 6 ends with תכלת וארגמן, from 10:9. They are consecutive lines of one fragment, so whatever stood between those two words occupied a single line.

The Masoretic arrangement asks for two and a half lines of writing in the space of one. It will not go in. That is the argument the editors make for this scroll, and it does not depend on a single reconstructed letter: it depends on where the extant text stands and how wide the lines are.

What this does not show

It shows that 10:5–8 as the Masoretic Text has them were not there. It does not by itself show that verse 9 stood before verse 5, or that verse 10 was missing: the fragment has room for the shorter text in several arrangements, and the one printed above is the editor's, chosen because the Greek has exactly that order. Between the extant text of 10:9 and that of 10:11 the sums do not decide anything — the Masoretic text with verse 10 and the scroll's text with the second half of verse 5 come to nearly the same length.

What the manuscript reads

Two adjacent words that are both extant are a join, and a join is testimony: whatever the Masoretic Text has between them did not stand in this copy. It is the only kind of omission a manuscript can attest, since an omission within a lacuna is the modern editor's and not the scribe's. Below, the words in blue are extant; the words in ochre are what the Masoretic Text sets between them.

Three of the five go with the Greek, one with the Masoretic Text, and one with neither. That is the texture of the thing: 4QJerᵇ's neighbour 4QJerᵈ is close to the text behind the Greek without being a copy of it.

4QJerb has no such join at all

Its extant text is 114 letters in 15 discrete runs, and no two of its words are adjacent in the manuscript. Everything above about Jeremiah 10 rests on the extent of the lacunae and nothing on two words standing next to each other. The two manuscripts are evidence of different kinds, and worth keeping apart.

The same test where it ought to find nothing

Three omissions in one manuscript is three data points, and no way to tell a finding from a habit of the method. The other four Jeremiah manuscripts from the Judaean Desert are of the Masoretic type, so they are the null: if the join test is reading the shape of a text rather than the shape of the damage, they should carry the bracketed pluses that 4QJerd omits.

Not one of the 42 bracketed pluses these four could answer for is missing from them, against 3 of 4 in the pair. Fisher's exact test gives p = 0.00026. The instrument is reading the edition.

Why this is a null and not silence

In the whole book they produce one join between them, 4QJerᶜ at 21:7, where the manuscript sets החרב against ביד and the Masoretic Text has ומן־הרעב between them. Stipp does not bracket it, so the Greek has it too: it is this manuscript’s own omission, most likely an eye slipping from one מן to the next. The test does find omissions here. It never finds one over a Masoretic plus.

One rule had to be added to get here. A single letter is not an anchor: the ETCBC writes a prefixed particle as a word of its own, and at 4QJera 14:4 the alignment paired the bet of the manuscript’s בארץ with the bet of the Masoretic בעבור ten words earlier, then reported the ten words between as excluded. Both anchors and the gap must now be two letters or more. It removes three of the four joins the Masoretic-type manuscripts had and none of 4QJerd’s, so it makes the contrast above narrower to claim, not wider.

How this was measured

The Masoretic Text is the ETCBC BHSA database. The scrolls are the ETCBC dss dataset, Martin Abegg's transcription, in which every sign carries a flag saying whether it is extant or restored; nothing on this page treats a restored sign as a witness. The Greek is Rahlfs, from the LXX dataset. Greek Jeremiah runs on its own versification: Greek 50 is Masoretic 43, while in chapter 10 the numbers agree and it is the verses themselves that differ.

For the question of which Masoretic words are absent from the Greek, the page follows Hermann-Josef Stipp, Textkritische Synopse zum Jeremiabuch, 15. korrigierte interne Auflage (2021), as parsed in the companion study. Every reading it is relied on for here was checked against Rahlfs directly.

Why the Greek is Rahlfs and not the Synopse's own panel

The Synopse prints the Greek beside the Hebrew colon by colon, which is a finer alignment than the verse-level one used here, and it would be the natural text to set. Checked against Rahlfs verse by verse over both passages, it agrees on 32 of 34. The exceptions are 10:5 and 10:9, and they fail in one particular way: each carries the other's Greek. Stipp's panel for 10:5 is 29% similar to Rahlfs at 10:5 and 99% similar to Rahlfs at 10:9, and the panel for 10:9 is the mirror of that.

That is where the two editions are transposed, and it is the fault of reading the panel rather than of the edition: Stipp marks the transposition himself, with his star and a margin cross-reference. But a Greek panel laid out against a Hebrew column running in Masoretic order cannot show the Greek's own order, and the Greek's own order is what the first section of this page is about. Rahlfs read as a text keeps it. So the Greek here is Rahlfs, and the Synopse is used for what only it can give: which Masoretic words the Old Greek lacks.

One caution about the apparatus citations quoted above. The parse takes Stipp's margin apart into spans, so the manuscript siglum and the place it stands at are reliable, while the wording around them is not necessarily whole. Nothing on this page rests on the wording of a marginal note.
